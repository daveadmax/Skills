# Research and evidence

## Research sequence

1. Define the exact market, geography, audience, and decision horizon.
2. Start with first-party sources: company sites, product documentation, annual reports, earnings material, public filings, customer research, and campaign case studies.
3. Add independent primary sources: regulators, official statistics, research organisations, trade bodies, and original surveys. Use reputable journalism or analyst work to establish context only when primary material is unavailable.
4. Inspect direct competitors and adjacent substitutes. Look for their offer, target audience, messaging, pricing, proof, distribution, customer experience, and evident strategic choices.
5. Record each claim as evidence, inference, or assumption. Cite the direct supporting source near the claim.

## What to look for

- Category demand, growth, structure, constraints, and shifts.
- Audience jobs, behaviours, occasions, frictions, language, and workarounds.
- Competitor norms, white space, and credible counter-moves.
- Organisational assets, limitations, capabilities, proof, and business model.
- Technology, cultural, economic, and regulatory dynamics that could alter the decision.

## Predictions and scenarios

Do not make a single unsupported prediction. Present 2–3 plausible scenarios when uncertainty materially affects the recommendation:

- **Base case:** what follows if current patterns continue.
- **Upside case:** what changes if a relevant advantage or adoption curve accelerates.
- **Downside case:** what changes if a constraint, competitor, or regulation bites.

For each scenario, give the signal to watch and the strategic implication. Describe forecasts as inferences, not facts.

## Citation discipline

Use direct Markdown links. Prefer recent sources for mutable facts and include the publication date when the timing changes the meaning. Do not cite search-result pages. Do not invent customer evidence, market sizes, performance metrics, or case-study outcomes.

# AI-writing patterns: editorial checklist

Use this as a diagnostic checklist, not as an authorship test. A single feature proves nothing; genre, language, accessibility, house style, and the source material can all explain it. Keep correct, purposeful writing even if it resembles an item below.

## Content patterns

- Inflated significance: claims about legacy, pivotal moments, broad trends, identity, or lasting impact that are not supported by a concrete fact.
- Generic analysis: a factual sentence followed by an empty interpretation (`reflecting`, `symbolizing`, `highlighting`, `contributing to`).
- Puffery: praise or travel/marketing language in place of evidence.
- Vague attribution: unnamed experts, observers, reports, critics, or `sources` presented as authority.
- Formulaic closure: a compulsory `challenges`, `future prospects`, or conclusion paragraph that adds speculation rather than information.
- Knowledge-gap fiction: statements that details are unavailable followed by guesses about what is likely true.
- Premature abstraction: opening with `X is a...` or `X refers to...` when the reader needs the concrete event, decision, or problem first.
- Unnecessary attribution theater: calling out categories of media coverage or a social-media presence as if this itself establishes credibility.

## Language patterns

- Clusters of elevated vocabulary such as `pivotal`, `crucial`, `vibrant`, `robust`, `underscore`, `showcase`, `foster`, `enhance`, `landscape`, `tapestry`, and `testament`.
- Avoidance of simple constructions (`is`, `has`, `uses`) in favor of `serves as`, `stands as`, `marks`, `features`, or `offers` without a gain in precision.
- Mechanical contrast (`not only X but Y`, `not X but Y`, `rather than Y`) and overworked triads.
- Excessive synonym swapping that obscures the subject or makes terminology inconsistent.

## Formatting patterns

- Title Case headings where sentence case fits the locale or house style.
- Repeated bold mini-headings, decorative lists, emoji headings, small tables that should be prose, or horizontal rules before every section.
- Overuse of em dashes. Preserve them when they suit the writer's established voice or the language's punctuation norms.
- Leftover chat, citation, template, or markup artifacts. Remove or repair them only after confirming the target format.

## Revision moves

| Pattern | Diagnose | Prefer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `The launch marks a pivotal moment...` | Says importance but not why | State the change, its scope, and its evidence—or remove it |
| `The tool serves as a hub that offers...` | Avoids plain verbs and hides action | `The tool combines...` or `The tool shows...` |
| `..., highlighting its importance` | Repeats the sentence's implied conclusion | End at the fact, or add an actual consequence |
| `Experts say...` | Unspecified authority | Name the source and its claim, or remove it |
| `Not just X, but Y` | Formulaic contrast | State Y directly; retain contrast only if it changes interpretation |
| `Despite its strengths, X faces challenges...` | Stock conclusion | Name the concrete tradeoff, risk, or limitation—or omit |
| `In conclusion / Overall` | Repeats without adding | End on the final meaningful assertion |
| `Key highlights:` plus bold bullets | Template formatting | Use prose, a real heading, or a list with a clear scanning purpose |

## QA questions before delivery

1. Could any sentence apply unchanged to a competing company, different town, unrelated project, or generic person? If so, make it specific or remove it.
2. Does every adjective, adverb, or evaluative verb earn its place through evidence, a stated viewpoint, or the requested voice?
3. Did the revision retain the original facts, caveats, commitments, and level of confidence?
4. Did any new implication slip in through a stronger verb, a causal connector, or an unnamed consensus?
5. Are lists, headings, typography, and punctuation useful in the target channel rather than merely polished-looking?
6. Does the text match the intended reader's knowledge and decision context?
7. Would a real author recognize their position and voice in the revision?

## What not to overcorrect

- Correct grammar, formal language, one transition word, curly quotes, Markdown, or a well-made list are not reliable signs of AI writing.
- Do not flatten a distinctive voice into generic minimalism.
- Do not introduce mistakes or arbitrary quirks to imitate human writing.
- Do not claim a rewrite will bypass AI detectors; detectors and human judgments have meaningful error rates.
